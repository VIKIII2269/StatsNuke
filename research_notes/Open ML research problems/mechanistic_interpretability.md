## Q1. Canonical open-problem lists and agendas: which items remain open in 2026?

### Takeaway
The main reference agenda is still Sharkey et al., "Open Problems in Mechanistic Interpretability" (arXiv 2501.16496, TMLR 2025). Neel Nanda's "200 Concrete Open Problems" (2022) is stale by its author's own account. Since mid-2024 the GDM team, Nanda's in particular, has pivoted from "ambitious" reverse-engineering toward "pragmatic" interpretability judged by proxy tasks. Late-2026 surveys still name evaluation methodology and benchmarks as the field's most pressing open problems.

### Cited Findings

It organizes open problems into (a) methods and foundations, (b) applications, and (c) socio-technical problems including policy and governance. It was later published in TMLR 2025. — [arXiv 2501.16496](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.16496); [TMLR listing](https://mlanthology.org/tmlr/2025/sharkey2025tmlr-open); [FAR.AI page](https://www.far.ai/research/open-problems-in-mechanistic-interpretability)

Progress is tracked in a public spreadsheet with "Existing Work" and "work in progress" columns. — [Alignment Forum post](https://alignmentforum.org/posts/LbrPTJ4fmABEdEnLf/200-concrete-open-problems-in-mechanistic-interpretability); [tracking spreadsheet](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/1oOdrQ80jDK-aGn-EVdDt3dg65GhmzrvBWzJ6MUZB8n4/edit)

It notes that different methods use incompatible metrics. (Seen in search snippet only.) — [arXiv 2611.08438](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.08438)

### Inferences
- **Which Sharkey et al. items remain open (inferred from 2026 literature):** All the big methods-and-foundations items are still open: the right unit of analysis (features vs. parameter components), faithful and complete circuit discovery, evaluation and ground truth, and scaling interpretation beyond manual effort. The 2026 surveys and critiques listed above still frame these as open, and no source claims any of them resolved.
- The old "200 problems" list is most useful for its toy-model, algorithmic-task and Othello sections, which remain good low-compute starter projects. Its SAE-free framing is outdated, and many of its "analyze a circuit in GPT-2" items have partial existing work.
- In practice, community taste in 2026 rewards work that (a) beats a strong simple baseline (linear probe, prompting, steering vector) on a well-defined proxy task, or (b) supplies ground-truth testbeds for evaluating methods. Both suit solo researchers.

## Q2. SAEs, transcoders, crosscoders, attribution graphs, absorption, SAE evaluation, SAEs vs. probes, and parameter decomposition

### Takeaway
SAEs are no longer seen as a default win. Multiple 2025–2026 papers find they rarely beat linear probes or simple steering baselines on downstream tasks, and random-init baselines score close to trained SAEs on standard metrics. Transcoders and cross-layer transcoders, which power attribution graphs, are now the favoured basis for circuits. Several problems are wide open and cheap to work on on small models: absorption, inconsistency across seeds, non-1D features, the faithfulness of attribution graphs, and automated graph interpretation. Parameter decomposition (APD → SPD) is a young alternative whose small-model scaling and toy validation are still open.

### Cited Findings
**SAEs vs. baselines**
- "Are Sparse Autoencoders Useful? A Case Study in Sparse Probing" (Kantamneni, Engels, Rajamanoharan, Tegmark, Nanda; arXiv 2502.16681; authors from training knowledge) tested SAE probes on 113 datasets under data scarcity, class imbalance, label noise and covariate shift. SAEs occasionally win on individual datasets, but SAE+baseline ensembles never consistently beat baseline-only ensembles. — [arXiv 2502.16681](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.16681.pdf)
- GDM's March 2025 post "Negative Results for SAEs on Downstream Tasks and Deprioritising SAE Research" is the public record of GDM deprioritising SAEs. A follow-up LessWrong post found SAE probes beat baselines only about 27% of the time in data-scarce settings, and null results for OOD and imbalanced data. — [GDM negative results post](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/4uXCAJNuPKtKBsi28/negative-results-for-saes-on-downstream-tasks); [SAE probing follow-up](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/NMLq8yoTecAF44KX9/sae-probing-what-is-it-good-for-absolutely-something)
- AxBench (arXiv 2501.17148) concludes that "even simple baselines outperform Sparse Autoencoders" for steering. — [arXiv 2501.17148](https://export.arxiv.org/pdf/2501.17148)
- "Do Sparse Autoencoders Generalize? A Case Study of Answerability" (arXiv 2502.19964) finds residual-stream probes beat SAE features in-domain, while generalization differs sharply across settings. — [arXiv 2502.19964](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2502.19964)
- A 2026 sanity-check paper (arXiv 2602.14111) found that random baselines nearly match fully trained SAEs on interpretability (0.87 vs 0.90), sparse probing (0.69 vs 0.72) and causal editing (0.73 vs 0.72). — [arXiv 2602.14111](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.14111)
- SAEBench (Karvonen et al., 2025, arXiv 2503.09532) is the standard multi-metric SAE benchmark (training knowledge, not re-verified this session). — [arXiv 2503.09532](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.09532)

**Absorption, splitting, consistency, geometry**
- "A is for Absorption" (Chanin et al.; arXiv 2409.14507; NeurIPS 2025 oral) shows feature absorption is caused by hierarchical features plus sparsity pressure. It occurs in every LLM SAE tested (hundreds of open-source SAEs), and varying SAE size or sparsity does not fix it. — [arXiv 2409.14507](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.14507v6); [NeurIPS 2025 oral](https://nips.cc/virtual/2025/oral/130303)
- Matryoshka SAEs (Bussmann et al., arXiv 2503.17547) train nested dictionaries and report reduced absorption, more disentangled latents, and better probing and concept-erasure scores. The evidence comes from the authors' own experiments on Gemma-2-2B and TinyStories, so independent replication is still pending. — [arXiv 2503.17547](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.17547v1)
- Paulo & Belrose, "Sparse autoencoders trained on the same data learn different features" (ICLR 2026), and "Sparse autoencoders do not find canonical units of analysis" (Leask et al., arXiv 2502.04878, ICLR 2025) both challenge the uniqueness of SAE features. The first came via a reference list; the second is training knowledge. — [snippet via 2606.06333](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.06333v1); [arXiv 2502.04878](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.04878)
- "Subspace-Aware Sparse Autoencoders" (arXiv 2606.06333, June 2026) questions the assumption that features are one-dimensional by learning decoder subspaces with group sparsity. — [arXiv 2606.06333](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.06333v1)
- Other 2026 SAE-architecture papers: "Size Doesn't Matter: Cosine-Scored Sparse Autoencoders" (arXiv 2606.15054) and "Interference Beyond Geometry in Concept Extraction" (arXiv 2609.35351); titles only seen. — [2606.15054](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.15054); [2609.35351](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.35351)
- Anthropic's Circuits Updates: (May 2026) features with similar top activations and unembeds can have different causal steering effects, and looking at downstream features helps tell them apart. (June 2026) "Turn-Averaged SAEs" train on residuals averaged over a conversation turn, because per-token features are numerous and often uninformative for safety analysis. — [May 2026 update](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2026/may-update/index.html); [June 2026 update](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2026/june-update/index.html)

**Transcoders and attribution graphs**
- "Transcoders Beat Sparse Autoencoders for Interpretability" (Paulo, Shabalin, Belrose, EleutherAI; arXiv 2501.18823) finds transcoder features significantly more interpretable than SAE features. It introduces skip transcoders, which lower reconstruction loss at no cost to interpretability, and recommends moving away from SAEs on MLP outputs. — [arXiv 2501.18823](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.18823)
- Anthropic's "Circuit Tracing" methods paper (Ameisen et al., 2025) and "On the Biology of a Large Language Model" (Lindsey et al., 2025) introduced attribution graphs over cross-layer transcoders (training knowledge, not re-verified this session). — [Methods](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2025/attribution-graphs/methods.html); [Biology](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2025/attribution-graphs/biology.html)
- Anthropic open-sourced the circuit-tracer library with Neuronpedia front-end integration. It initially supported Gemma-2-2B and Llama-3.2-1B, and Qwen3-4B was added later (the search summary wrote "Llama-3.1-1B", but no such model exists, so 3.2-1B is the likely intended model). Anthropic invited the community to analyze unexamined graphs. — [Anthropic announcement](https://anthropic.com/research/open-source-circuit-tracing); [PyPI circuit-tracer](https://pypi.org/project/circuit-tracer/); [Neuronpedia blog](https://www.neuronpedia.org/blog/circuit-tracer)
- Neuronpedia frames attribution graphs as circuit hypotheses that must be validated with interventions. — [Neuronpedia graph info](https://neuronpedia.org/graph/info)
- Manual interpretation is the bottleneck: one LessWrong post estimates about 2 hours per prompt for an experienced tracer and proposes automating probe prompting across many graphs. — [LessWrong post](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/zQqGhKPqaCBZZDCge)
- A 2026 paper (arXiv 2601.22594) argues that Integrated Gradients is noisy and expensive on deep models, reports gains from a different attribution method, and finds neuron-level bases more faithful than sparse-dictionary bases. — [arXiv 2601.22594](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.22594)
- Anthropic's Oct 2026 update introduces the "J++ lens", a cluster-wise Jacobian lens that is a drop-in replacement for the J-lens and R-lens. It reportedly surfaces reasoning-relevant latent variables more reliably, with code, lenses and a Neuronpedia demo released. — [Oct 2026 update](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2026/october-update/)
- "Weight-sparse transformers have interpretable circuits" (arXiv 2511.13653, Nov 2025; believed to be OpenAI, Gao et al., but authorship not confirmed in snippet) trains models whose weights are sparse, making circuits small by construction. — [arXiv 2511.13653](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.13653)
- "Sparse Transformer: a Controlled Testbed for Mechanistic Interpretability" (arXiv 2610.19907, Oct 2026); title only seen. — [arXiv 2610.19907](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.19907)

**Parameter decomposition**
- Stochastic Parameter Decomposition (Bushnaq, Braun, Sharkey, Goodfire; arXiv 2506.20790, v1 June 2025, v2 Sept 2025) replaces APD, which was too compute-heavy and hyperparameter-sensitive. SPD decomposes each weight matrix into rank-one subcomponents with causal importance estimated by stochastic masking. Code is at goodfire-ai/spd. — [arXiv 2506.20790](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.20790v2); [Goodfire write-up](https://www.goodfire.ai/papers/stochastic-param-decomp); [AF post](https://alignmentforum.org/posts/yjrpmCmqurDmbMztW/paper-stochastic-parameter-decomposition)

**Tooling releases relevant here**
- Gemma Scope 2 (DeepMind, 19 Dec 2025) provides SAEs and transcoders on every layer of all Gemma 3 sizes (270M–27B), adds skip-transcoders and cross-layer transcoders, and has a separate suite for chat models (refusal, CoT faithfulness). It reportedly involved about 110 PB of data and more than 1T parameters trained. There is a Neuronpedia demo. Whether it includes crosscoders is unconfirmed. — [DeepMind blog](https://deepmind.google/blog/gemma-scope-2-helping-the-ai-safety-community-deepen-understanding-of-complex-language-model-behavior); [Gemma Scope docs](https://ai.google.dev/gemma/docs/gemma_scope)

### Inferences — problem cards (solo-tractable)
1. **Beat-the-probe benchmark for SAE/transcoder features on a new proxy task.** *Open because:* negative results (2502.16681, AxBench, 2602.14111) cover probing and steering, but not every use case: unsupervised discovery of unknown concepts, OOD auditing, or hypothesis generation. *Solo action:* pick a task where you can't label in advance (e.g., find the latent that predicts a spurious shortcut) and compare Gemma Scope 2 SAE/transcoder features against probes, PCA, difference-of-means, random-init SAEs and prompting. *Compute:* Gemma 3 270M/1B with Gemma Scope 2 fits on one consumer GPU (training-knowledge estimate). *Success:* a pre-registered metric (AUROC, OOD gap, auditing hit-rate) with a random-SAE control, per the 2602.14111 sanity check.
2. **Absorption and hierarchy in a toy model with known ground truth.** *Open because:* absorption is universal and not fixed by scaling (2409.14507). Matryoshka's fix has not been independently replicated, and its interaction with transcoders and CLTs is unclear. *Solo action:* build synthetic data with planted hierarchical features, then compare vanilla, TopK, BatchTopK, Matryoshka and subspace-aware SAEs (2606.06333) and transcoders on recovery of the ground-truth tree. *Compute:* CPU or one GPU in hours. *Success:* absorption score (Chanin metric) and recovery metrics against planted features.
3. **Seed and run consistency of dictionaries.** *Open because:* SAEs trained on the same data learn different features (Paulo & Belrose, ICLR 2026), and there are no canonical units (2502.04878). *Solo action:* train N seeds of small SAEs or transcoders on Pythia-70M or Gemma 3 270M, measure feature matching, and test whether a consensus or ensembling procedure yields stable features that are more causally useful. *Compute:* 1 GPU, days. *Success:* cross-seed match rate and whether stable features score higher on downstream tasks.
4. **Faithfulness audit of attribution graphs.** *Open because:* graphs are explicitly hypotheses (Neuronpedia), attribution methods are contested (2601.22594), and CLT error nodes hide computation. *Solo action:* use circuit-tracer on Gemma-2-2B or Qwen3-4B, or Gemma 3 transcoders, to build graphs for a family of prompts with known mechanisms (e.g., IOI, two-hop recall). Check graph-predicted intervention effects against actual ablations, and compare the CLT basis with a neuron basis. *Compute:* 1 GPU (24 GB is comfortable for 2B; training-knowledge estimate). *Success:* correlation between predicted and actual intervention effects, and the fraction of logit effect routed through error nodes.
5. **Automating attribution-graph interpretation.** *Open because:* about 2 hours per prompt of expert time (LessWrong zQqGhKPqaCBZZDCge). *Solo action:* an LLM-agent pipeline (Claude or open models) that labels supernodes and proposes and tests interventions, evaluated against hand-annotated Neuronpedia graphs. *Compute:* mostly API calls, plus 1 GPU. *Success:* agreement with human annotations, and validated intervention rate.
6. **SPD on small real models and toy circuits.** *Open because:* SPD (2506.20790) is validated mainly on toy models; scaling to real language models, hyperparameter robustness, and comparison with transcoder circuits are open. *Solo action:* run goodfire-ai/spd on a 1–4-layer TinyStories model or a modular-arithmetic transformer and check whether its components match known circuits. *Compute:* 1 GPU. *Success:* recovery of known mechanisms, and faithfulness of the rank-one components under ablation.
7. **Weight-sparse models as ground truth for evaluating SAE/CLT methods.** *Open because:* weight-sparse transformers (2511.13653) and the "Sparse Transformer testbed" (2610.19907) provide models with small known circuits, but they have barely been used to score dictionary-learning or attribution methods. *Solo action:* train a small weight-sparse model on a synthetic task, then check whether SAEs, transcoders and attribution graphs recover the true circuit. *Success:* precision and recall of circuit edges.
8. **Non-1D and multi-token features.** *Open because:* most SAEs assume 1D features (2606.06333), and per-token units are often uninformative (Anthropic June 2026 turn-averaged SAE). *Solo action:* find and characterise circular or multi-dimensional features (days, months, numbers) in Gemma 3 1B, then compare group-sparse SAEs with turn- or span-averaged SAEs. *Success:* reconstruction and interpretability at matched sparsity.
9. **Lens benchmarking (logit, tuned, J, J++, R lens).** *Open because:* Anthropic released the J++ lens in Oct 2026 with only internal comparisons. *Solo action:* independently benchmark these lenses on small open models for latent multi-hop reasoning variables. *Success:* the accuracy with which each lens decodes known intermediate variables.

## Q3. Interpretability of non-language models: TabPFN, time-series FMs, structured-sequence transformers, Othello/chess world models, sports-event sequences

### Takeaway
Tabular foundation-model interpretability is a fresh frontier with first papers in 2026 (probing, grandmother neurons, causal head localisation in TabPFN), and many easy wins remain, notably SAEs/transcoders on TabPFN and in-context-learning circuits. Time-series FM interpretability is at the probing stage. Othello/chess remain classic toy testbeds. Sports-event sequence transformers exist (Seq2Event, a soccer foundation model, EventGPT), but no interpretability studies of them were found, which makes them a genuinely unexplored, low-compute testbed.

### Cited Findings
- "TabPFN Through The Looking Glass" (Gupta, Sethi, Kumar; arXiv 2601.08181, Jan 2026; NeurIPS 2026 listing) presents itself as the first mechanistic and representational analysis of TabPFN v2. On synthetic tasks with known structure, regression coefficients and intermediate arithmetic terms are strongly decodable in middle layers, and answer signals become linearly recoverable earlier than the output head aligns them. — [arXiv 2601.08181](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.08181v1); [NeurIPS 2026](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2026/160449)
- "In Search of Grandmother Cells: Tracing Interpretable Neurons in Tabular Representations" (arXiv 2601.03657) reports neurons with moderate, statistically significant saliency and selectivity for high-level concepts in tabular models. — [arXiv 2601.03657](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.03657)
- "Where Computation Lives Inside TabPFN: Causal Localisation of Attention Head Function" (ICML 2026) presents itself as the first causal mechanistic analysis of a tabular FM (TabPFN 2.5). Contrastive activation steering fails to transfer across samples, which the authors attribute to in-context learning relying on context-dependent attention rather than stable parametric directions. — [ICML 2026 listing](https://icml.cc/virtual/2026/71542)
- "On the Internal Semantics of Time-Series Foundation Models" (arXiv 2511.15324) uses probing to study seven concepts spanning stochastic, structural and spectral behaviour (e.g., trend, variance shift). Earlier work by Wiliński et al. (2025) used CKA for layer-wise similarity in TSFMs. — [arXiv 2511.15324](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.15324)
- Othello-GPT: Li et al. (arXiv 2210.13382) found board state was recoverable non-linearly. Nanda et al. (arXiv 2309.00941) showed it is linear in "mine vs. theirs" terms and causally steerable. Hazineh (arXiv 2310.07582) found the causal effect depends on layer depth. — [2210.13382](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.13382); [2309.00941](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.00941); [2310.07582](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.07582v2)
- Chess (training knowledge, not re-verified this session): Karvonen 2024 found board-state and player-skill probes in chess-GPT (arXiv 2403.15498). Karvonen et al. 2024 used Othello and chess as SAE benchmarks with ground-truth board features (arXiv 2408.00113). Jenner et al. 2024 found evidence of learned look-ahead in Leela Chess Zero (arXiv 2406.00877). — [2403.15498](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.15498); [2408.00113](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.00113); [2406.00877](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.00877)
- Sports-event sequence models exist: Seq2Event (Simpson et al., KDD 2022, transformer next-event prediction), "A Foundation Model for Soccer" (arXiv 2407.14558), EventGPT (arXiv 2512.17266, a GPT-style model with a 22-player and game-state context block followed by autoregressive event tokens), an axial transformer for forecasting action totals (arXiv 2511.18730), and dense event representations (arXiv 2606.09327, which notes transformer use in football event analytics "remains limited"). The search found none that applies probing or mechanistic interpretability. — [Seq2Event](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/458099/1/KDD22_paper_CReady_v20220606.pdf); [2407.14558](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2407.14558); [2512.17266](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.17266); [2511.18730](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.18730v1); [2606.09327](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.09327v1)

### Inferences — problem cards
1. **SAEs/transcoders on TabPFN.** *Open because:* published TabPFN work is probing, neurons and head localisation, and no dictionary-learning or circuit-tracing study was found. *Solo action:* train SAEs on TabPFN v2 residuals over synthetic priors and check whether latents correspond to the generating-function components (linear coefficients, noise level, feature relevance). *Compute:* TabPFN is small and runs on 1 GPU or even CPU for small tables (training-knowledge estimate). *Success:* match against known data-generating parameters.
2. **ICL algorithm identification in TabPFN.** *Open because:* steering fails to transfer because computation is context-dependent (ICML 2026). *Solo action:* test whether TabPFN implements something like ridge regression, kNN or Bayesian model averaging in-context, using planted tasks and activation patching, in the style of the "transformers learn in-context by gradient descent" literature. *Success:* behavioural and internal match to a candidate algorithm across tasks.
3. **TSFM concept circuits beyond probing.** *Open because:* TSFM work is at the probe and CKA stage (2511.15324). *Solo action:* causal patching or SAEs on a small open TSFM (e.g., Chronos-small or TimesFM-small; model availability is training knowledge) for trend, seasonality and variance-shift concepts. *Success:* causal steering of forecasts along identified directions.
4. **A sports-event GPT as a world-model testbed (Othello-GPT analogue).** *Open because:* no interpretability work on event-sequence models was found. *Solo action:* train a 2–8-layer transformer on public event data (e.g., StatsBomb open data; availability is training knowledge) with next-event prediction. Probe for latent game state that is never given directly: score, possession, time remaining, momentum, and implied win probability. Then test linearity and causal steering, and train SAEs where ground-truth state variables provide evaluation labels, as with chess and Othello SAE benchmarks (2408.00113). Bonus: compare the model's implied outcome probabilities with betting odds, a calibration angle that ties into Q4. *Compute:* a few hours on 1 GPU for a small model (estimated). *Success:* probe accuracy for latent state, intervention-consistent predictions, and SAE recovery of known variables.
5. **Othello/chess as SAE and attribution-graph ground truth.** *Still open:* applying 2025–26 methods (CLTs, attribution graphs, SPD, Matryoshka) to models with known board state, as a benchmark for whether new methods recover true variables. *Compute:* trivial. *Success:* board-state recovery metrics.

### Gaps
- No 2025–2026 Othello-GPT or chess mech-interp papers surfaced in this session's search, so the newest work there is unverified.
- No interpretability of sports-event models was found. This is an absence in one search, not proof of none.
- No source confirmed which TSFMs are the most convenient open checkpoints for interpretability in 2026.

## Q4. Interpretability of probabilistic reasoning, uncertainty, calibration and forecasting in LLMs

### Takeaway
2025–2026 work is mostly probe-based. Linear probes on hidden states predict verbalized confidence beyond log-probs, confidence probes transfer cross-lingually, and layer-agreement methods match probes in-distribution. Mechanistic accounts of *how* uncertainty is computed are scarce, beyond earlier "entropy/confidence-regulation neurons" work, and none were found for forecasting. That leaves an open, low-compute niche.

### Cited Findings
- Kumaran et al. (arXiv 2603.17839, Mar 2026) find that linear probes on cached representations explain variance in verbal confidence beyond token log-probabilities. — [arXiv 2603.17839](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.17839v2)
- Badash et al. (arXiv 2603.22299, Mar 2026) propose single-forward-pass uncertainty from cross-layer agreement that matches probing in-distribution across three models, motivated by probes being high-dimensional and hard to transfer. — [arXiv 2603.22299](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22299)
- Wang et al. (arXiv 2505.18575, May 2025) find that lower response uncertainty goes with higher linear-probe accuracy on internal representations. — [arXiv 2505.18575](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.18575v1)
- Kyriakou et al. (arXiv 2605.31220, May 2026) report that confidence probes trained on one language transfer to unseen languages. — [arXiv 2605.31220](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.31220)
- EAGLE (Xiao et al., AAAI 2026, arXiv 2509.01564) aggregates intermediate-layer beliefs into a confidence score. — [arXiv 2509.01564](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.01564v2)

## Q5. Tooling and small open-weight models for solo work

### Takeaway
The 2026 solo stack: TransformerLens or nnsight for hooks and patching; SAELens plus Gemma Scope 2 (SAEs, transcoders and CLTs for Gemma 3, 270M–27B) for dictionaries; circuit-tracer plus Neuronpedia for attribution graphs (Gemma-2-2B, Llama-3.2-1B, Qwen3-4B); goodfire-ai/spd for parameter decomposition; and Pythia or TinyStories-scale models for training your own.

### Cited Findings
- Gemma Scope 2 covers all Gemma 3 sizes (270M–27B), with SAEs and transcoders on every layer, skip-transcoders and CLTs, chat-model variants, and a Neuronpedia demo (released 19 Dec 2025). — [DeepMind blog](https://deepmind.google/blog/gemma-scope-2-helping-the-ai-safety-community-deepen-understanding-of-complex-language-model-behavior); [docs](https://ai.google.dev/gemma/docs/gemma_scope)
